Case history template
  1. Signalment: species, breed, age, sex, body weight, colour, identification.
  2. Owner's complaint in the owner's own words, and how long it has been present.
  3. History: onset, progression, appetite, water intake, urine, faeces, milk yield or production change, other animals affected.
  4. Management: housing, feed, vaccination and deworming record, recent purchase or travel.
  5. Clinical examination: demeanour, TPR, mucous membranes, CRT, hydration, lymph nodes, then system by system.
  6. Provisional diagnosis and differentials, discussed with your supervisor.
  7. Tests advised and their results.
  8. Treatment as directed by the supervising veterinarian, outcome and follow-up.
Animal observation record
  • Date, time, animal ID, species and location.
  • Behaviour and posture: alert, dull, standing, recumbent, head position.
  • Eating, drinking, rumination (ruminants), urination and defaecation.
  • Gait, any lameness, skin and coat, discharges, swellings.
  • Write what you see, not what you assume.
Case presentation structure
  1. One-line summary: signalment and main complaint.
  2. Relevant history.
  3. Examination findings: positive findings first, then the important negatives.
  4. Problem list and differential diagnoses with reasons.
  5. Tests, diagnosis and the plan decided by the supervisor.
  6. What you learned, and one question you still have.

Saved only on this device. Do not write owner names or other private details here.

Clinical rotation planning
  • List your rotations (medicine, surgery, gynaecology, ambulatory and so on) as given by your college.
  • For each one write 3 things you want to learn and ask your supervisor what is expected.
  • Revise the related theory beforehand and schedule it in the Study Planner.
  • At the end of each rotation write one log entry above.
Practical learning objectives
  • Take a clear, complete history and record it neatly.
  • Handle and restrain animals safely, under supervision.
  • Perform and record a general clinical examination.
  • Explain your reasoning for a problem list.
  • Explain a plan to an owner in plain language.
Supervisor discussion questions
  • What would make you more worried or less worried about this case?
  • What else should I rule out, and how?
  • What can I safely do under your supervision, and what must wait?
  • What should I read before the next similar case?
Professional communication tips
  • Introduce yourself and your role clearly to owners.
  • Avoid jargon with owners. Say "inflammation of the udder", not only "mastitis".
  • Never promise outcomes. Refer treatment questions to the supervising veterinarian.
  • Be honest when you do not know. Say "let me check with my supervisor".
  • Keep records neat, dated and honest.
